# Ideal Client Profile

Practical sales intelligence for who to approach first, based on what the
product actually does well and where it actually has gaps (see
`PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md`). No demographic statistics are used here — none
have been gathered, so none are claimed.

## 1. Primary ideal client

A **private/independent school** (Early Years through Secondary or
A-Level, similar in shape to the Aurelia Heights demo) that:

- Currently has **no website, or one that visibly hasn't been touched in
  years** (broken layout, no mobile version, outdated term dates still
  showing). This is the single strongest buying signal this product
  matches, because the value of "we'll build you something dramatically
  better" is largest against a genuinely bad or absent baseline —
  against a school that already has a decent site, the pitch is weaker
  and the sale is slower.
- **Already runs a WhatsApp Business number or an active
  Facebook/Instagram page** for parent communication. This matters
  specifically because the product's contact architecture is built
  WhatsApp-first (see `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §3, §10) — a school already
  living in that channel will recognize the fit immediately, rather than
  needing to be sold on WhatsApp as a concept.
- **Has some usable photography already** — even ordinary phone photos of
  the campus, classrooms, and events, taken for social media. This is the
  product's biggest current gap (no real photography ships with it — see
  `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §6), so a client who can supply even mediocre
  real photos removes the single largest blocker to a genuinely
  launch-ready site. A client with *zero* usable photography turns a
  content-configuration engagement into a photography-commissioning
  engagement, which is a different, longer, costlier sale.
- **Is actively recruiting** — mid-size enough to have real admissions
  competition (i.e., parents are choosing between several schools, not
  simply enrolling at the only option in the area), because that's where
  a credible web presence actually changes a parent's decision rather
  than being a nice-to-have.
- **Has a single clear decision-maker** who can approve a purchase without
  a lengthy multi-stakeholder procurement process — see §6.

## 2. Secondary ideal clients

- **Newer/growing academies and institutes** (opened within the last
  5–10 years) actively trying to establish credibility against
  longer-established competitors. A polished site is disproportionately
  valuable to a school still building its reputation, since it's one of
  the few credibility signals a young institution can control directly.
- **Small colleges/institutes offering a narrower program** (vocational,
  technical, or specialized tracks) — the product's academics/admissions
  architecture generalizes to this reasonably well, though the "Programs"
  and "Curriculum" language in the current demo is worded for a general
  K-12 academy and would need real rework, not just a find/replace, for
  a genuinely different institution type (a vocational college's
  "curriculum" section looks nothing like Aurelia Heights's seven-subject
  accordion).
- **A second campus/branch of an existing institution** wanting a
  consistent but distinct site — this is close to the product's actual
  proven use case (three brand presets, same underlying architecture),
  so it's a comfortable fit.

## 3. Poor-fit clients

- **A school that already has a genuinely good, modern website.** There's
  no compelling reason for them to switch, and competing on "ours is
  slightly better" is a weak, slow sale against this product's actual
  strengths.
- **A school with no digital presence at all** — no WhatsApp Business
  number, no active social media, staff uncomfortable with basic web
  tools. The gap between "no digital presence" and "using a modern
  website" is large, and this product doesn't include the training,
  handholding, or ongoing management support that gap would require. This
  is a services/consulting sale, not a product sale.
- **A large institution with an existing IT department or an existing
  vendor relationship for its website.** Procurement will be slow,
  requirements will diverge from this product's actual shape (they may
  want a genuine CMS, staff logins, integration with existing systems —
  all explicitly out of scope per `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §8), and the
  decision won't rest with one person.
- **A public/government school** where the budget and approval process
  for something like this is unclear or where free/mandated
  government-provided systems may already be in play. Different sales
  motion entirely, likely a poor first-customer choice regardless of
  ultimate feasibility.
- **A school unwilling or unable to supply real photography or real
  content** (leadership bios, actual program details, actual
  achievements) within a reasonable timeframe. Per `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md`
  §6, this product's rebranding is explicitly a config-plus-content
  process — a client who can't or won't provide the content half leaves
  the engagement stuck indefinitely, not delivered late.

## 4. Signs a school is likely to buy

- They mention their current website unprompted, with visible
  frustration ("it's embarrassing," "it hasn't been updated in years,"
  "parents can't find our contact info").
- A staff member is already manually managing a WhatsApp Business account
  or Facebook page for the school — meaning someone already understands
  the value of a digital presence and just lacks the next piece.
- They can produce photos or describe content readily when asked ("we
  have photos from Founders' Day," "our headteacher can write something")
  — a sign the content-authoring half of the engagement won't stall.
- They ask practical, near-term questions early ("how long would this
  take," "can parents apply through it," "does it work on phones") rather
  than abstract ones — a sign they're evaluating a real purchase, not
  browsing.
- A single person (headteacher, director, or a designated
  admissions/marketing staff member) is clearly empowered to say yes.

## 5. Signs a school is unlikely to buy

- The conversation routes immediately to "we'd need to discuss this with
  the board/proprietor/ministry" with no timeline given.
- They ask for features explicitly out of this product's scope early and
  insist on them — a student portal, online fee payment, a full CMS
  they can edit themselves without any developer involvement (see
  `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §8). This signals a mismatch in what they think
  they're buying, not just a hard negotiation.
- They can't produce any usable photography or content when asked, and
  show no urgency about getting any — the gap identified in §3 above.
- They already paid for a website within roughly the last year or two and
  seem to feel some sunk-cost attachment to it, even if it's mediocre.
- Repeated, hard-to-schedule meetings with no clear single point of
  contact — a proxy for the "no clear decision-maker" problem in §3.

## 6. Who the actual decision-maker may be

Varies by institution size, and getting this wrong wastes time, so it's
worth asking directly early rather than assuming:

- **Small/independent school**: usually the headteacher/director
  personally, sometimes jointly with the school's proprietor/founder if
  those are different people (common for privately-owned academies).
- **Larger or more established school**: may route through a deputy head
  or a designated administration/marketing staff member, but final
  budget approval likely still sits with the head or a small governing
  body — worth confirming who actually signs off before treating a
  staff-level contact as the buyer.
- **Multi-campus institution**: could be centralized (one decision-maker
  for all campuses) or per-campus — ask rather than assume, since this
  materially changes deal size and sales cycle length.

## 7. What the decision-maker cares about

- **Credibility and trust signals for parents evaluating the school** —
  this is the product's central pitch and its actual strength (see
  `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §4, §10).
- **Not looking worse than competing schools' websites.** Competitive
  positioning against specific other local schools is often a more
  persuasive frame than abstract "professional website" language.
- **Reducing repetitive admissions/contact questions** — a decision-maker
  who's personally fielding "what are your fees," "where are you
  located," "how do I apply" questions by phone will value the
  admissions/contact architecture concretely, not abstractly.
- **Cost and ongoing commitment.** A static site with no CMS means no
  recurring software fee, but it also means the school can't edit content
  themselves without a developer — some decision-makers will see the
  "no CMS" fact as a limitation, not a feature, and that objection should
  be anticipated (see §9).
- **Timeline** — how fast can this actually go live, and is that
  realistic given how much content/photography the school itself needs
  to supply (an honest answer depends heavily on that, not just on
  development time — see `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §6).

## 8. What parents/students care about

(Relevant context for the decision-maker's pitch, even though parents
aren't the buyer)

- Whether the school looks legitimate, current, and well-run — a
  first-impression trust signal, not a feature checklist.
- Being able to find practical information fast on a phone: fees process,
  how to apply, where the school actually is, how to reach someone by
  WhatsApp or phone without hunting for it.
- Seeing real students, real classrooms, real campus life — which is
  exactly where the product's current placeholder-photography gap will
  be most visible to this audience, and worth being upfront about in
  early client conversations rather than letting a client discover it
  late.
- Students specifically (for older-year admissions) may care whether the
  school looks "modern" in a way that reflects on them socially — a real
  but harder-to-quantify factor.

## 9. Objections the institution may have

- **"We don't have professional photos."** Directly addresses the
  product's real current gap — have a clear, honest answer ready (a
  photography day, phone photos as an interim, or the placeholder system
  as a genuinely presentable interim state) rather than being caught
  off-guard by this.
- **"Can we update it ourselves?"** No, not without a developer — this is
  a real, structural limitation of a static site with no CMS (see
  `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §6, §8), not a minor caveat. This objection
  should be anticipated and answered honestly, including what a content
  update actually costs/takes going forward.
- **"How is this different from a template I could buy for $50?"** The
  honest answer is the information architecture (nine real pages, not
  one), the accessibility/mobile work, and — if a support/maintenance
  relationship exists — that a $50 template comes with none of that or
  any ongoing help. This should not be answered by inventing performance
  or design claims that haven't been benchmarked against alternatives.
- **"We already have a Facebook page — why do we need a website too?"**
  A website is durable, searchable, and not subject to a social
  platform's algorithm or the parent needing an account to see it — a
  real, honest distinction, not a dismissal of what they're already
  doing right.
- **"What happens if we want a student portal / online payments later?"**
  Honest answer: that's a larger, separate scope (see
  `PRODUCT_OVERVIEW.md` §8) — worth naming directly rather than
  implying the current product already leads there.
- **Price sensitivity**, especially against free/DIY website builders.
  The honest differentiator is the same as above (architecture, content
  structure, and support) — not a claim that this is simply cheaper.
